Make a font from an image

No printer needed. Write the alphabet on a blank sheet — one group per line — photograph it, and get an installable TTF. Everything runs in your browser.

Before you write — read this

On this route there are no printed guides, so we work out where the baseline is by looking at which character is which. That only works if what you wrote matches what you tell us. Copy these four lines exactly, in this order.

Line 1 · 26 capitals

ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ

Line 2 · 26 lowercase

abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz

Line 3 · 10 digits

0123456789

Line 4 · 14 punctuation marks

.,!?'"-():;&@#

  • · Made a mistake? Cross it out and carry on, then put a ~ at that position in the line text. That tells us to skip the crossed-out blob instead of counting it as a character — otherwise every character after it shifts by one.
  • · Leave a gap between characters so they don't touch. Touching letters get merged into one glyph.
  • · Let g p q y j hang below the line you are writing on — that is what makes them look right.
  • · Dark pen on white paper. Ballpoint is fine, pencil is not.
  • · Skipping characters entirely is fine — just delete them from the line text too.

Example. If your sheet reads ABCDEFGHIJK / LMNOPQRSTUV / WXYZ, that's three lines, not one — enter all three.

Converting handwriting to a font without a printer

Most tools that turn handwriting into a font start by asking you to print something. That is a reasonable request — a printed grid is the easiest way to know where every letter sits — but it rules out anyone without a printer, which is most people most of the time. This route removes that step. Plain paper, a pen you already own, and the camera in your pocket are enough.

What you give up is a small amount of precision, and it is worth understanding where it goes. A font does not store letters as pictures; it stores each glyph in a coordinate system where y = 0 is the baseline. A descender is simply a negative number. Get the baseline wrong and the letters are all still there, but the g sits level with the o and the whole thing looks subtly broken — which is exactly what makes most home-made fonts look home-made.

On the free printable font template that baseline is printed in every box, so it is measured. Here it has to be recovered from the writing itself: we group the ink into lines, find the dominant bottom edge of the letters in each line, and use the lowercase row to establish the x-height. That is why this page insists on knowing which characters you wrote and in what order — the identity of each shape is the prior that makes the whole reconstruction possible.

The five things that decide whether it works

  • Dark ink, white paper. Ballpoint, gel or fineliner with a medium tip. Pencil is grey, low-contrast and smudges; very fine 0.2 mm pens can vanish at photo resolution.
  • Characters must not touch. Two letters that touch are read as one shape and become one glyph. Leave a visible gap.
  • A clear gap between lines. The lines of writing are separated before anything else happens. Rows that overlap vertically get merged.
  • Even light, no shadow. A shadow across the page is the single most common cause of missing characters. Shoot near a window, not under a single overhead lamp with your phone between the lamp and the paper.
  • Let the descenders hang. Write g p q y j so they drop below the line you are writing on. If you float them, the font will float them too.

Questions people actually ask

Can I make a font from a photo of my handwriting?
Yes. Write the alphabet on plain paper with a dark pen, photograph it with a phone, and this page cuts each character out of the photo and assembles them into an installable TTF font. There is no printer, no account and no upload — the photo is processed by JavaScript running on your own machine.
How is this different from the printed template route?
The printed template has a baseline and an x-height line drawn inside every box, so the exact vertical position of each letter is measured rather than inferred. On plain paper those lines do not exist, so we work out the baseline from the shapes themselves — which requires that you tell us which characters are on each line. The result is very close, but the printed template is still the most precise route if you happen to have a printer.
What do I actually have to write?
Four lines: A–Z, then a–z, then 0–9, then the punctuation marks. You do not have to fit each group on a single line — if you run out of room, start a new line and add a matching line in the text boxes on this page. Writing large is better than cramming.
Why do I have to type out what I wrote?
Because the order is what tells us which blob in the photo is which character. On a printed template the box position carries that information; on plain paper nothing does. If the text you enter does not match the sheet — a missed character, an extra doodle — every character after that point is labelled with the wrong letter, and the mistake is silent. That is why the page shows you a thumbnail of every character with its label, so you can catch it in one scan.
What kind of photo works?
A flat, evenly lit phone photo taken from directly above. The page is forgiving about resolution and mild perspective, but not about three things: dark ink on white paper (pencil is too grey), characters that do not touch each other, and a clear gap between the lines of writing. Shadows falling across the page are the most common cause of a poor result.
I made a mistake halfway down the line. Do I start over?
No. Cross the mistake out, carry on writing, and put a ~ at that position in the line text. That tells us to throw the crossed-out blob away instead of counting it as a character. Without it every character after the crossing-out shifts by one.
Does my photo get uploaded anywhere?
No. Everything — decoding the photo, finding the lines of writing, cutting out the characters, tracing them into outlines and assembling the TTF — happens inside your browser. Nothing is sent to a server, there is no account, and we never see your handwriting. You can put the page in aeroplane mode after it loads and it still works.
What can I do with the font once I have it?
It is an ordinary TrueType font. Install it on Windows or macOS and it appears in Word, Google Docs, Canva, Photoshop, Figma and anything else that lists your system fonts. It covers 76 characters: A–Z, a–z, 0–9 and 14 punctuation marks.

Installing the font you make

On Windows, right-click the .ttf and choose Install — Microsoft's instructions are in Manage fonts in Windows. On a Mac, double-click the file and click Install, or use Font Book — Apple's Font Book guide covers every method. Restart any app that was already open before it will see the new font.
