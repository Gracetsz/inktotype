What happens to your photo

Four steps, all of them on your own machine. First the four black corner squares are located and the photo is un-warped, so a picture taken at an angle becomes a flat, square-on sheet — this is also what makes the print scale irrelevant, because the real size is solved from the markers themselves. Then the grid is cut into 76 boxes, each box is separated into ink and paper, and each patch of ink is traced into a vector outline. Finally those outlines are packed into a TrueType file with the right metrics.

The step that matters most is one you cannot see: every glyph is measured against the printed baseline in its box, using one scale for the whole sheet. A font stores each glyph in a coordinate system where y = 0 is the baseline and a descender is a negative number. Tools that cut letters out of a plain grid have to guess that position, and the usual guess — align every letter by its own bounding box — puts the tail of a g level with the bottom of an o. That single mistake is what makes most home-made fonts look wrong. Here it is measured, not guessed.

Writing the sheet well

  • Sit each letter on the solid line. That line is the baseline, and it is the reference for everything. Floating a letter above it makes that one character sit high in the font.
  • Let g p q y j drop below it. Descenders are supposed to hang. Write them the way you normally would.
  • Use a medium-tip dark pen. Ballpoint, gel or fineliner. Pencil is too grey and it smudges; 0.2 mm pens can disappear at photo resolution.
  • Stay inside the box. Ink that crosses into the neighbouring box gets cut off at the border.
  • Write it once, normally. Carefully drawing each letter produces a font that looks like careful drawing. The point is to capture your hand, not your best calligraphy.

Before you photograph it

All four black corner squares must be completely inside the frame — that is the one hard requirement, and the most common reason an upload fails. Lay the sheet flat, shoot from directly above, and use even light. A shadow falling across the page, or your own phone shadowing the paper under an overhead lamp, will cost you characters. Resolution and mild perspective are forgiving; missing corners and shadows are not.

Questions people actually ask

How do I make my own font from my handwriting?
Print the free 76-box template, write one character per box with a dark pen, photograph the whole sheet, and upload the photo here. Your browser cuts each character out, traces it into an outline and assembles an installable TTF. It takes about ten minutes and costs nothing.
How long does it actually take?
Writing the sheet is the slow part — most people take five to eight minutes for all 76 characters. Printing, photographing and building the font take under two minutes between them. If you rush the writing it shows in the font, so this is the one step worth slowing down for.
Do I need to print at 100% scale?
No. The four black squares in the corners tell us the real printed size, so any scale works as long as the whole sheet with all four squares is in your photo. The sheet has a wide safety border so default printer margins cannot clip the squares.
What happens if some boxes fail?
The review screen flags them and you can redraw any individual character in the browser rather than reprinting the sheet. Characters you leave out are simply absent from the font — the other 70-odd still work.
Why does the review screen matter so much?
Because writing a character in the wrong box is the one mistake nothing can detect. That box has ink, it passes the quality check, and it is in the right position — so a d drawn in the b box becomes the letter b in your font, silently. Scanning the thumbnails against their labels once is the only place that gets caught.
Should I trace the grey letter printed in each box?
No. The grey character shows you where the letter should sit, not what it should look like. Trace it and you get a hand-drawn copy of our typeface instead of your own handwriting. You can switch the grey letters off before printing.
Is my handwriting uploaded to a server?
No. The photo is decoded, cut up, traced and assembled into a TTF by JavaScript running on your own machine. There is no account and no upload. Once the page has loaded you can go offline and the whole process still completes.
What if I do not have a printer?
Two other routes need no printer. You can write the alphabet on plain paper and photograph that, or write each character straight onto the screen with a stylus. Both produce the same 76-character TTF.

Installing your font

On Windows, right-click the .ttf and choose Install — see Manage fonts in Windows. On a Mac, double-click and click Install, or use Font Book. Restart any app that was already open before it will see the new font. After that it works anywhere a font works — Word, Google Docs, Canva, Photoshop, Figma.
