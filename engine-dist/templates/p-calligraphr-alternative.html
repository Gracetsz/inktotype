Where Calligraphr is the better tool

Three cases, and in all of them you should pay them rather than use this.

  • You want joined-up cursive. Real cursive needs ligatures — shapes that connect one letter to the next. Without them, joined handwriting comes out as a row of disconnected letters that were drawn as if they would connect. Calligraphr Pro does ligatures; this does not.
  • You want it to stop looking repetitive. One fixed shape per character means every e in a paragraph is pixel-identical, which real handwriting never is. Calligraphr's multiple variants per character are the fix, and they matter more the more text you set.
  • You need accents or a non-Latin script. 76 characters covers English. Anything with accented vowels, Cyrillic, Greek or a larger character set needs the 600-character limit.

Where the free one is enough

The common case, honestly. Most people making a handwriting font want one font, of their own normal print handwriting, in English, to use on cards, notes, a personal project or a bit of design work. For that, 76 characters is not a stripped-down free tier — it is the whole job, and the ceiling never comes up.

Two other things tend to decide it. There is no account here, so you can go from curious to finished font without registering or handing over an email address. And nothing is uploaded: the photo is decoded, cut up, traced and assembled into a TTF by JavaScript on your own machine, so your handwriting never leaves your device. Whether that second point matters is up to you — handwriting is more personal than most of what people upload without thinking.

There is also no printer requirement. Photograph plain paper or draw with a stylus and you never print anything.

Nothing to sign up for, so the cost of finding out whether the result is good enough for you is ten minutes and a sheet of paper.

Questions people actually ask

Is there a free alternative to Calligraphr?
Yes. InkToType makes a 76-character handwriting font — A–Z, a–z, 0–9 and 14 punctuation marks — free, with no account and no watermark. Calligraphr's free plan covers 75 characters per font; its Pro plan raises that to 600 and costs $10 for one month, or $6 a month on a six-month upgrade.
What does Calligraphr do that InkToType does not?
Several things, and they are real. Calligraphr Pro supports up to 15 variants of each character so the same letter does not repeat identically, ligatures for letter pairs that should join, per-character spacing adjustment, up to 600 characters — which is what you need for accented and non-Latin alphabets — and server-side backup of your work. InkToType has none of these. If you want joined cursive or a language beyond basic Latin, Calligraphr is the better tool and it is not close.
What does InkToType do that Calligraphr does not?
Three things. It needs no account — you never register or give an email address. It runs entirely in your browser, so your handwriting is never uploaded anywhere. And it offers three ways in rather than one: a printed template, a photo of plain paper with no printer at all, or drawing directly on screen with a stylus.
Which one should I use?
If you want one font of your own everyday handwriting for personal use, start with the free tool — 76 characters covers everything you normally type in English, and you will know in ten minutes whether you like the result. If you need variants, ligatures, accents or a non-Latin script, pay for Calligraphr Pro. They are not really competing for the same job.
Do I need a printer for either of them?
Not for InkToType. The printed template gives the most precise letter positions, but you can also photograph handwriting on plain paper, or write with a stylus and never touch paper at all.
Are the fonts the same format?
Both produce standard installable fonts. InkToType gives you a TrueType .ttf, which installs on Windows and macOS and works in Word, Canva, Photoshop and anything else that reads your system fonts.
