The route with no paper in it

The other two ways of making a font here start with ink on paper and end with a camera. This one skips both. You write directly into the coordinate system the font will use, which removes every source of error that lives between the pen and the file: no print scale to solve, no perspective to un-warp, no shadow to confuse the ink from the paper, no character accidentally written in the wrong box.

That precision is the reason to choose this route, and the trade is worth being honest about. A font drawn on glass looks like a font drawn on glass. Strokes are cleaner and more even than a real pen's, and the small irregularities that read as handwriting — ink pooling at a change of direction, a letter that came out slightly differently because it was the fortieth one you wrote — are mostly absent. If you want your actual handwriting, the printed font template or the photo route will get closer. If you want a clean, controlled hand-lettered font — or you simply have an iPad and no printer — draw it here.

What you write on

A font does not store letters as pictures. It stores each glyph in a coordinate system where y = 0 is the baseline, and a descender is simply a negative number. The solid line on the pad is that y = 0. Everything you draw is measured against it, which is why sitting each letter on the line matters more than drawing it beautifully — a gorgeous g that floats above the baseline will float in every word you ever type.

The dashed line above it is the x-height. Lowercase letters without ascenders — a, c, e, m, n, o, r, s — should reach it and stop. Consistency against that one line does more for how the finished font looks than the quality of any individual letter.

Devices, ranked by how well they work

  • iPad + Apple Pencil. The best combination available. Pressure and low latency both work, and the result is close to writing on paper.
  • Drawing tablet (Wacom, Huion, XP-Pen). Just as accurate, though writing while looking at a separate screen takes a few characters to get used to. Screen tablets avoid that entirely.
  • Android tablet with S Pen, or a Surface with a pen. Pressure is supported and results are good.
  • A finger on a touchscreen. Works, and is fine for a deliberately chunky, casual font. Fine detail is hard.
  • Mouse or trackpad. Works, but expect the result to look drawn rather than written. If this is all you have, the photo route will almost certainly give you a nicer font.

Questions people actually ask

Can I draw my own font online?
Yes. This page gives you a writing pad with a baseline and an x-height line printed on it. Write each of the 76 characters once with a stylus, drawing tablet or finger, and download an installable TTF at the end. Nothing is printed, photographed or uploaded.
Do I need an Apple Pencil?
No, but it makes a large difference. Any pressure-sensitive stylus works — Apple Pencil, a Wacom pen, a Samsung S Pen, an MPP stylus on a Surface. Pressure is mapped to stroke width, so a stylus gives you thick and thin strokes the way a real pen would. A finger works and a mouse works, but a mouse in particular produces strokes that look drawn rather than written.
Will my palm resting on the screen ruin it?
No. Palm rejection switches on automatically the moment a pen touches the pad — after that, touch input is ignored and only the stylus draws. You do not have to turn anything on.
How is this different from photographing handwriting on paper?
The metrics are exact here. Nothing is photographed, so nothing can be distorted by perspective, lighting or print scale, and the baseline is a real line on the pad rather than something reconstructed from the shapes. What you give up is the character of a real pen on real paper — ink pooling, paper texture, the small inconsistencies that make handwriting look handwritten.
How many characters do I have to draw?
76: A–Z, a–z, 0–9 and 14 punctuation marks. You can skip any you do not need — they are simply absent from the finished font, and the rest still work. Most people take ten to fifteen minutes for the full set.
What do the two lines on the pad mean?
The solid line is the baseline: sit each letter on it, and let g, p, q, y and j hang below it. The dashed line is the x-height — the top of a lowercase x. Keeping your lowercase letters consistent against that line is what makes the finished font look even rather than ransom-note.
Can I redo a character I am not happy with?
Yes, at any point. Navigate back to it and clear the pad, or redraw over it. Nothing is final until you build the font, and you can rebuild as many times as you like without starting over.
Does anything get uploaded?
No. The strokes stay in your browser, the font is assembled in your browser, and the TTF is saved straight to your downloads folder. There is no account and no server involved in making the font.

Installing the font you make

On Windows, right-click the .ttf and choose Install — see Manage fonts in Windows. On a Mac, double-click and click Install, or use Font Book. Restart any app that was already open before it will see the new font.
